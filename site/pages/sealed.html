2.1What is not sealed yet

Injection, sample id and retention time are shown beside each record as context; they are not inside the seal. The alpha app keeps retention time unsealed and does not keep sample or method yet. Sealing sample and method metadata inside the record is on the roadmap.

3The fingerprint

r{{sn}} is the SHA-256 of the payload, written as 64 lowercase hex characters:

{{rG}}

Change one character of any field and the fingerprint changes completely. Anyone who has the five fields can recompute it:

printf '%s' '{{payload}}' | sha256sum

On macOS, use shasum -a 256. A fingerprint is not encryption: anyone who can guess the fields can try them (section 9).

BasisSHA-256, FIPS 180-4.

4The link to the previous record

Each record also stores a link: hn is the SHA-256 of hn−1, rn and n, written one after another as text (lowercase hex, then the decimal position), starting from 64 zeros at the start of record. 4.1How a checker reads the links

A checker recomputes each record’s link from the previous link that record stores, its recomputed fingerprint and its position, and compares the result with the link the record stores, which is also the link the next record carries. So a changed value fails that record’s fingerprint and signature and breaks one link: the link into the next record. The later records still pass their own checks.

The change still shows across the whole history: replayed from 64 zeros over the recomputed fingerprints, the last link comes out different, so the head no longer matches the anchored fingerprint (section 7).

5The signature (demo)

Each fingerprint is signed with ECDSA P-256 over its 32 bytes. On these pages, records 1 to {{N}} were signed when the site was built, by a demo key whose private half was then discarded. Its public key ships with the pages that check it. That shows the method but is not independent evidence: in use, the key would be published outside the lab’s system. {{demoSig}}

In the record checker, an edited record’s signature still fails after the history is rewritten, but only because the demo key was discarded. In the alpha, whoever holds the one server key could re-sign an edited record; only a fingerprint anchored outside the lab beforehand would still disagree.

A digital signature on a record’s bytes is not an electronic signature by a person under 21 CFR Part 11.

6Corrections appended with a reason

A record is never edited in place. A correction is a new record, appended after the newest one, with the corrected value, its own fingerprint, link and signature, the reason for the change and the number of the record it corrects. The original stays as sealed and keeps passing its checks. The records already anchored still match the anchored fingerprint; the new head is not anchored until the next batch.

On these pages the reason and the reference sit beside the seal, not inside it, because the alpha’s payload has five fields. The alpha console documents its own payload in its Method panel. The alpha app has no corrections yet.

7Anchoring a batch

Anchoring writes a batch fingerprint, with a time, somewhere the lab does not control, so that a later rewrite of the history shows. On these pages the batch fingerprint is the last link of the batch: the whole history replayed from 64 zeros up to record {{AN}}. It is stored on the page as the anchored fingerprint (simulated, no network call).

{{ancG}}

In the alpha, anchoring is manual. The project has a small registry contract for {{network}}: whoever deploys a copy can write a batch fingerprint to it by hand. No shared registry exists yet, and the console’s publishing screen is paused while the site is redesigned, so every anchor on this site and in the console is simulated.

Only a fingerprint goes on the network, never the values. A registry entry shows that a fingerprint existed by a given time. It does not show who the lab is, and it does not keep the records.

8What a pass and a fail show

A pass shows
The five sealed fields are unchanged since the record was sealed (fingerprint); the holder of the key signed that fingerprint (signature); the record sits where it was sealed in the history (link); and the batch is the one that was anchored (anchor).
A pass does not show
That the result was right when it was captured: the right sample, a suitable system, correct integration. Who made the record, since signatures are not yet per analyst. Anything about fields outside the seal, such as sample, method or retention time. With the key and the anchor shipped on the same page, it shows the method, not independent evidence.
A fail shows
Something changed after sealing, and where: which record no longer gives its fingerprint, and which link it breaks. A rewritten history shows as a signature that fails or a head that no longer matches the anchor.
A fail does not show
Who changed it, when, or which value is right. Nor that the change was improper: a correction appended with a reason is the proper route, and it passes.

9Privacy on public networks

A fingerprint hides nothing that can be guessed. A plain SHA-256 of a few guessable fields (an instrument id, a measurement type, a peak area in a known range, a capture time to the second) can in principle be matched by trying likely values.

Today the alpha writes only a batch or chain fingerprint to a public network, never the values and never a single record’s fingerprint, and only by hand.

Roadmap Salted (hiding) commitments in place of plain fingerprints, so that what is published reveals nothing without the salt the lab keeps. Later, zero-knowledge proofs, so that a lab can show a property, such as “within specification” or “reviewed before release”, without disclosing the value.
